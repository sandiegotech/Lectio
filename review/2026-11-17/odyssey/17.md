# The Odyssey · Book XVII · The Beggar at His Own Door
Grade: B
Argos, Melanthius and the footstool are all well told, and Argos's death keeps Homer's exact wording. Two lines change the sense, and several smaller slips need fixing.

## Issues
- `seg-0025` · fidelity · medium
  > Servants lose half their worth the moment there is no master's hand over them; Zeus takes the better half of a man away
  Two separate sayings are merged here. The first in Greek is "servants no longer care to do their proper work when masters no longer rule them". Only the second is about Zeus taking half a man's worth. As written, "half" is said twice.
  Fix: "Servants stop doing honest work the moment their masters are no longer over them; Zeus takes away half a man's worth the day he becomes a slave."
- `seg-0024` · fidelity · medium
  > the hound Odysseus had bred himself before he sailed for Troy, though he had never lived to get any use of him
  "Never lived to" says Odysseus died. The Greek is "he never had the joy of him", because he sailed first.
  Fix: "though he sailed before he had any joy of him."
- `seg-0051` · read-aloud · medium
  > The rest I will leave to me and to heaven.
  This is ungrammatical. Greek: "all this will be my care, and the gods'."
  Fix: "The rest is my concern, and the gods'."
- `seg-0011` · fidelity · low
  > they would find their wedding short and their death quick
  Homer's pair is "swift death and bitter wedding". "Short" loses the bitterness.
  Fix: "their death would come quick and their wedding bitter."
- `seg-0037` · voice · low
  > Odysseus stood firm as a rock; the blow from Antinous did not so much as rock him on his feet
  The word "rock" comes twice in one sentence.
  Fix: "the blow from Antinous did not so much as stagger him."
- `seg-0040` · fidelity · low
  > one of the high-born young men spoke up
  The Greek epithet is "arrogant" (*hyperēnoreontōn*). "High-born" softens it.
  Fix: "one of the arrogant young men spoke up".
- `seg-0031` · read-aloud · low
  > no one calls in a beggar to eat him out of house
  The idiom is cut short.
  Fix: "no one calls in a beggar to eat him out of house and home."
- `seg-0044` · register · low
  > their own corn and wine lie untouched in their houses
  An American reader takes "corn" to mean maize.
  Fix: "their own grain and wine".
- `seg-0050` · read-aloud · low
  > to look after the property — yours and what is mine
  Fix: "to look after your living and mine."
- `seg-0032` · register · low
  > God forbid.
  This is a monotheist idiom in a poem that elsewhere says "the gods". The Greek is "may a god not bring this about".
  Fix: "Heaven forbid."
- `seg-0015` · register · low
  > Out at the steading
  "Steading" again (see Books XIV and XV).
  Fix: "Out at the farm".

## Best lines
- `seg-0026` "Argos, in the very moment he had seen his master again in the twentieth year, was taken by the darkness of death."
- `seg-0028` "a beggar cannot afford to be ashamed."
