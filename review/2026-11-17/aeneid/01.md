# Aeneid · Book I · The Storm, and Carthage
Grade: B
A strong, faithful prose book with the great lines intact; one misreading in the proem and a handful of softened or flattened phrases.

## Issues
- `seg-0001` · fidelity · high
  > carry his gods into Latium — the gods from whom the Latin people came
  The Latin race, the Alban fathers and Rome descend from Aeneas, not from his gods ("genus unde Latinum"; Dryden: "From whence the race of Alban fathers come", of his line).
  Fix: "and carry his gods into Latium. From him came the Latin people, the Alban fathers, and the high walls of Rome."
- `seg-0001` · fidelity · medium
  > Hard was the road that battered him over sea and land, for Juno's relentless rage hounded him
  "vi superum" (by the power of the gods) is dropped, so only Juno is named. The inverted "Hard was the road" is also a leftover archaism.
  Fix: "He was battered hard over land and sea by the power of the gods, because of cruel Juno's unforgetting rage;"
- `seg-0038` · fidelity · medium
  > the poor queen, already doomed, with her eye on the coming ruin
  "pesti devota futurae" means she is given over to the ruin to come. "With her eye on" says she foresees it, which she does not.
  Fix: "the poor queen, already doomed, given over to the ruin to come,"
- `seg-0019` · fidelity · medium
  > monstrous Rage, perched on a heap of weapons
  "Furor impius". In a poem built on pietas, "impious" is the point and should not be dropped (Dryden keeps "impious war").
  Fix: "godless Rage, sitting on a heap of savage weapons,"
- `seg-0019` · register · medium
  > Faith and Vesta, and Romulus with his brother Remus, will lay down the law.
  Readers take "lay down the law" as an idiom meaning "scold". "Iura dabunt" means they will give laws.
  Fix: "will give the world its laws."
- `seg-0021` · fidelity · medium
  > or like one of those Thracian women who outrun the swift rivers
  This drops the proper noun and the horses: "qualis equos Threissa fatigat Harpalyce" (Dryden names Harpalyce).
  Fix: "or like Thracian Harpalyce, who rides her horses to exhaustion and outruns the swift river Hebrus."
- `seg-0017` · register · medium
  > betrayed (it's unbelievable) for the anger of one goddess
  "Infandum!" is an outcry ("unspeakable!"). The chatty parenthesis shrinks it.
  Fix: "betrayed (a thing not to be spoken) to the anger of one goddess"
- `seg-0014` · voice · low
  > Someday, perhaps, even this will be something we look back on and enjoy remembering.
  "Forsan et haec olim meminisse iuvabit" is one of the most quoted lines in the poem, and here it is padded out.
  Fix: "Someday, perhaps, it will be a joy to remember even this."
- `seg-0014` · register · low
  > Pull yourselves together and let go of this fear and grief.
  "Revocate animos" is a commander's call. "Pull yourselves together" sounds modern and scolding.
  Fix: "Call back your courage, and put away this fear and grief."
- `seg-0010` · fidelity · low
  > Three times, four times luckier were the men who died
  "Beati" means blessed or happy, not lucky.
  Fix: "Three times, four times blessed were the men who died"
- `seg-0010` · read-aloud · low
  > The winds, as if formed into a column, came pouring out
  "Velut agmine facto" is a military image, and "formed into a column" reads stiffly.
  Fix: "The winds, like an army in battle order, came pouring out"
- `seg-0024` · fidelity · low
  > a name known, once, above the clouds
  The added "once" softens his claim. "Fama super aethera notus" is in the present tense.
  Fix: "known by fame above the sky."
- `seg-0028` · fidelity · low
  > walking to the temple of Athena, who would not be appeased
  Virgil's name is Pallas (Minerva), not Athena. The edition also wrote "Pallas Athena" in seg-0006. Keep the poem's names.
  Fix: "walking to the temple of hostile Pallas"; in seg-0006, "Pallas".
- `seg-0023` · fidelity · low
  > which is why it's called Byrsa, 'the Hide.'
  The translation of the name is a gloss added to the sentence, and belongs in the enrichment layer.
  Fix: "which is why the place is called Byrsa."
- `seg-0038` · register · low
  > began little by little to wipe out Sychaeus
  "Wipe out" reads as killing.
  Fix: "began little by little to blot out the memory of Sychaeus"
- `seg-0035` · form · low
  > I remember — I was only a child — when Teucer came to Sidon
  Dido's speech continues from seg-0034, but the new paragraph has no opening quotation mark.
  Fix: open the segment with a quotation mark.
- `seg-0035` · fidelity · low
  > Even Troy's enemy used to praise the Trojans
  The enemy is Teucer himself ("ipse hostis"), and the generic phrase loses that irony.
  Fix: "Teucer himself, Troy's enemy, used to praise the Trojans"

## Best lines
- "there are tears for the way of things, and the troubles of mortal men touch the heart."
- "The work hums; the honey breathes of thyme."
