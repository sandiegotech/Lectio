## Recurring patterns
1. **"Virtù" is handled three ways.** It is defined inside the sentence in I, kept untranslated through XXIV, then turned into "strength," "power," "worth," "ability," and "military virtue" in XXV–XXVI, exactly where the book pits virtù against fortune and calls back to Moses, Cyrus, and Theseus. Pick one policy (keep "virtù" everywhere, glossed once in the margin) and apply it from I to XXVI.
2. **Editorial glosses and translations sitting in the text.** "virtù — a man's own skill and nerve" (I), "by losing" (XII), "the Parlement — the high court" (XIX), and the English renderings of Virgil (XVII) and Petrarch (XXVI) all read as Machiavelli's own words. Move glosses to the margin or enrichment, and set verse translations apart; restore the verse line breaks in both quotations.
3. **Words in senses a modern reader does not carry, and name drift.** "militia" for soldiering (VIII), "wretched" for *tristi* (XVII, XVIII), "argue weakness" (XX), "under cover" (XVIII), and "France" for Roman Gaul (IV, XIX). Spellings drift too: Vaila/Vailà, Forli/Forlì, and "Allesandria" carried over from the 1908 text.

## Verdict
This is an excellent edition: faithful clause by clause, more accurate than Marriott in a dozen places (the fetters of III, "miserabile" in XXI, "when things outside are quiet" in XIX, the four men of XIII), and written in one steady, plain, forceful voice that keeps every famous line recognizable. There are no high-severity errors and no section needs redoing. It is ready to ship once the seven medium fixes are applied. The two that matter most are the consistent handling of "virtù" (I, XXV, XXVI) and the two meaning slips: the counterfactual Pisa in VII and "let those go" in XV. The low items can go in the same pass or wait.
