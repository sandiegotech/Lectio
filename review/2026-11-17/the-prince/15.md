# The Prince · Chapter XV · What Princes Are Praised or Blamed For
Grade: B
The great opening (effectual truth, learning how not to be good) is rendered exactly; the list paragraph has an unclosed aside and one clause that can be read backwards.

## Issues
- `seg-0002` · fidelity · medium
  > and if he cannot, he may let those go with less concern.
  The source ("vi si può con meno respetto lasciare andare") means he may give himself over to those lesser vices with less hesitation; "let those go" reads as giving the vices up, the opposite sense.
  Fix: "and if he cannot, he may indulge in them with less concern."
- `seg-0002` · read-aloud · medium
  > another a miser — I use the Tuscan word, since in our tongue an avaricious man is still one who craves to get by plunder, while we call a miser the man who holds back too hard from using what is his own; one is a giver, another grasping
  The dash opens an aside that never closes, so the list resumes inside it and the reader loses the thread; the source sets this off in parentheses.
  Fix: "another a miser (I use the Tuscan word, since in our tongue an avaricious man is still one who craves to get by plunder, while we call a miser the man who holds back too hard from using what is his own); one is a giver, another grasping"

## Best lines
"it has seemed to me more fitting to go straight after the effectual truth of the thing than after the imagination of it."
"So it is necessary for a prince who wants to keep his state to learn how not to be good, and to use that knowledge, or not use it, as necessity requires."
