# Hamlet · Act III
Grade: B
This is the edition's best act. "To be, or not to be", the nunnery scene and the closet scene mostly keep Shakespeare's words and clear only what needs it. But a handful of the most famous lines are reworded, several puns are explained away, and the rhymed play-within-the-play loses its rhymes.

## Issues
- `seg-0137` · fidelity · high
  > The lady protests too much, I think.
  Perhaps the most quoted line in the act, and "methinks" is universally understood. Keep it.
  Fix: The lady protests too much, methinks.

- `seg-0025` · fidelity · high
  > To sleep, perhaps to dream—ay, there's the catch,
  "There's the rub" is a fixed phrase in current English. "Perchance" can become "perhaps", but "rub" must stay.
  Fix: To sleep, perchance to dream—ay, there's the rub,

- `seg-0025` · fidelity · low
  > and enterprises of great pitch and moment,
  The text silently switches to the Q2 reading. The base text has "pith", the familiar phrase.
  Fix: and enterprises of great pith and moment,

- `seg-0237` · fidelity · medium
  > O, my offense is rank, it stinks to heaven;
  A famous line, and "smells" is current.
  Fix: O, my offense is rank, it smells to heaven;

- `seg-0310` · fidelity · medium
  > blown up with his own bomb;
  "Hoist with his own petard" is a living idiom, and the rewrite throws it away.
  Fix: hoist with his own petard;

- `seg-0223` · fidelity · low
  > It's now the very witching hour of night,
  The famous line is lightly misquoted; the source has "time".
  Fix: 'Tis now the very witching time of night,  (or: It's now the very witching time of night,)

- `seg-0087` · fidelity · medium
  > to kill so fine a calf there.
  "Capital" puns on the Capitol in Polonius's line before it. "Fine" kills the joke.
  Fix: to kill so capital a calf there.

- `seg-0097` · fidelity · medium
  > Do you think I meant something coarse?
  "Country matters" is the famous bawdy pun, and here it is spelled out as its meaning.
  Fix: Do you think I meant country matters?

- `seg-0208` · fidelity · medium
  > though you can vex me, you cannot play upon me.
  "Fret" is both the frets of an instrument and "to vex". Without the pun the speech loses its point.
  Fix: though you can fret me, you cannot play upon me.

- `seg-0126` · fidelity · medium
  > honored, beloved; and, lucky, one as kind
  "Haply" means "perhaps". "Lucky" is wrong in meaning and does not fit the grammar.
  Fix: honored, beloved; and perhaps one as kind

- `seg-0119`, `seg-0125`, `seg-0130` · form · medium
  > here bowing to your mercy, / we beg your patient hearing.  …  for a woman's fear and love hold equal measure, / either nothing at all, or to the extreme.
  The Mousetrap is written in rhymed couplets, a stiff old-fashioned style set apart from the play around it. The edition breaks the rhyme in places (clemency/patiently, quantity/extremity, distrust/must, tend/friend, try/enemy) and pads it in others ("once it's ripe and free"). It also drops the source's italics for the Prologue.
  Fix: keep the couplets as written, changing only words that are truly dead: "For us, and for our tragedy, / here stooping to your clemency, / we beg your hearing patiently." Restore "But fall unshaken when they mellow be".

- `seg-0076` · read-aloud · medium
  > with the very keenest of your soul
  "Keenest" does not work as a noun here.
  Fix: with the very closest watching of your soul  (source: "the very comment of thy soul")

- `seg-0188` · fidelity · medium
  > But is there nothing following at the heels of this mother's amazement? Out with it.
  "Out with it" is added; it is not in the source.
  Fix: delete "Out with it."

- `seg-0150` · fidelity · low
  > So do women take their husbands—for better, for worse.
  The gloss is written into the line. The source reads "So you mistake your husbands", playing on "Still better, and worse" just before it.
  Fix: So you mis-take your husbands.

- `seg-0218` · fidelity · low
  > They lead me on to the very top of my folly.
  This mis-glosses the line. "They fool me to the top of my bent" means "they make me play the fool as far as I can go".
  Fix: They make me play the fool to the top of my bent.

- `seg-0078` · fidelity · low
  > I must seem idle.
  "Idle" means "mad". Adding "seem" points the reader the wrong way.
  Fix: I must be idle.

- `seg-0279` · voice · low
  > and glut yourself on this swamp?
  "Moor" is still a current word, and a moor is not a swamp.
  Fix: and batten on this moor?  (or: and gorge yourself on this moor?)

- `seg-0302`, `seg-0304` · voice · low
  > flattering ointment … spread the manure … Put on a virtue
  Each is an unneeded swap in a famous passage. The source has "flattering unction", "compost" and "Assume a virtue".
  Fix: restore "unction", "compost" and "Assume".

- `seg-0304` · fidelity · low
  > the devil of our evil ways,
  This silently adopts an editor's conjecture ("devil"); the base text has "Of habits evil".
  Fix: of evil habits,

- `seg-0109` · register · low
  > Anon comes in a fellow … She seems loth and unwilling awhile
  The dumb-show stage direction is left wholly in Early Modern English, unlike every other direction the edition touches.
  Fix: Soon a man comes in … She seems reluctant for a while

- whole act · voice · low
  "Ay" is kept throughout this act ("Ay, my lord", "Ay, truly"), but Acts I–II change it to "Yes". Pick one rule for the whole play. Keeping "Ay" is the lighter touch.

## Best lines
- `seg-0025`: "To be, or not to be" keeps nearly every word; only "contumely", "fardels", "bourn" and "bodkin" are cleared. This is the model for the whole edition.
- `seg-0168` (Act III sc. 2): "I watched him very closely." is a light, exact clearing of "I did very well note him."
