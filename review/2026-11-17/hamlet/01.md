# Hamlet · Act I
Grade: B
Every speaker, every scene, and the line breaks are intact. Meaning is sound almost everywhere. But this is a re-translation, not a light-touch edition: plain modern English that needed no help is reworded line after line, and several famous lines are bent out of shape.

## Issues
- `seg-0090` · fidelity · high
  > but I have that within which goes beyond all show—
  One of the play's best-known lines, rewritten with no need. The source reads "which passeth show"; the only friction is "-eth".
  Fix: but I have that within which passes show,

- `seg-0090` · fidelity · medium
  > Seems, madam? No, it is. I don't know "seems."
  "I know not 'seems'" is famous and clear. The rewrite flattens the retort.
  Fix: Seems, madam? No, it is. I know not "seems."

- `seg-0113` · fidelity · medium
  > He was a man, take him all in all.
  This misquotes the source, "take him for all in all," and the full stop cuts the line off from "I shall not look upon his like again."
  Fix: He was a man, take him for all in all,

- `seg-0136` · fidelity · medium
  > An expression more in sorrow than in anger.
  A famous phrase, and "countenance" is still a current word. The swap gains nothing.
  Fix: A countenance more in sorrow than in anger.

- `seg-0202` · fidelity · medium
  > more honored in the breaking than the keeping.
  This famous line is rewritten. "Breach" and "observance" are both current English.
  Fix: more honored in the breach than the observance.

- `seg-0202` · fidelity · medium
  > and born to it, it is a custom
  "To the manner born" is a phrase readers still use, and it is lost here.
  Fix: and to the manner born, it is a custom

- `seg-0298` · fidelity · medium
  > Rest, rest, troubled spirit.
  "Perturbed spirit" is famous and fully current.
  Fix: Rest, rest, perturbed spirit.

- `seg-0096` · fidelity · medium
  > as a sun-god to a satyr;
  A proper noun is dropped and replaced by its gloss. The source reads "Hyperion to a satyr", and the satyr stays, so the pairing should stay too.
  Fix: Hyperion to a satyr;

- `seg-0252` · fidelity · medium
  > in this distracted head.
  "Globe" carries Shakespeare's pun on the head, the world, and the theatre. "Head" spells out the gloss and kills the pun.
  Fix: in this distracted globe.

- `seg-0292` · form · medium
  > Hic et ubique? [Here and everywhere?] Then we'll shift our ground.
  A gloss in brackets is pasted into the spoken line, and the source's italics are gone.
  Fix: _Hic et ubique?_ Then we'll shift our ground.  (leave the gloss to the margin/enrichment)

- `seg-0296` · fidelity · medium
  > to put on an antic, madman's manner),
  The gloss is smuggled into the verse. The source reads "To put an antic disposition on".
  Fix: to put an antic disposition on),

- `seg-0050` · read-aloud · medium
  > that same frown, once, when in a furious parley / he struck the sledded Poles down on the ice.
  The sentence loses its verb. The source reads "So frown'd he once", but the edition leaves a fragment after "That was the very armor he had on…".
  Fix: So he frowned once, when in an angry parley / he struck the sledded Poles upon the ice.

- `seg-0075` · fidelity · medium
  > the imperial widow of this warlike state,
  A "jointress" holds property jointly; she is a partner in the throne. "Widow" describes her past instead of her new title and misses Claudius's point.
  Fix: the imperial partner of this warlike state,  (or keep "jointress")

- `seg-0056` · fidelity · medium
  > and the moist moon, / whose pull governs Neptune's ocean empire,
  Two glosses are written into the verse: "moon" for "star", and "pull governs … ocean" for "influence … stands".
  Fix: and the moist star, / upon whose influence Neptune's empire stands,

- `seg-0058` · fidelity · medium
  > which, by good luck, foreknowing might avoid—
  Here "happily" means "haply" (perhaps), not "by good luck", so the gloss is wrong.
  Fix: which, perhaps, foreknowing might avoid—

- `seg-0183` · fidelity · medium
  > you'll show me up a fool.
  This drops Polonius's third "tender", the punchline of his pun. "Show me up" is also British slang for "embarrass", which muddies the sense.
  Fix: you'll tender me a fool.  (the line already explains "tender" twice above)

- `seg-0170` · fidelity · low
  > This above all: to your own self be true,
  The famous line is lightly altered. "Thine own self" is the one place where keeping the pronoun is worth it.
  Fix: This above all: to thine own self be true,

- `seg-0170` · voice · low
  > for the clothes often proclaim the man,
  An unneeded rewrite of a famous line; "apparel" and "oft" read fine.
  Fix: for the apparel oft proclaims the man,

- `seg-0163` · fidelity · low
  > but as this body matures,
  The metaphor is flattened. "This temple waxes" is the image, and the only friction is "waxes".
  Fix: but as this temple grows,

- `seg-0004`, `seg-0007`, `seg-0011`, `seg-0013`, `seg-0029`, `seg-0030`, `seg-0038`, `seg-0112`, `seg-0137`, `seg-0140`, `seg-0208`, `seg-0216`, `seg-0274` · voice · medium
  > The same. / a quiet watch / tell them to hurry / Nonsense, nonsense / besiege your ears / Exactly like / a fine king / flushed / Most steadily / gesture / Take your hands off / only wild and whirling words
  A light-touch edition would leave all of these alone. The source lines ("He." / "quiet guard" / "bid them make haste" / "Tush, tush" / "assail your ears" / "Most like" / "a goodly king" / "red" / "Most constantly" / "courteous action" / "Hold off your hands" / "but wild and whirling words") are clear or need only one word changed. In "besiege" and "fine" the sense shifts.
  Fix: restore the source wording, changing only "Tush" (to "Tut, tut") and "goodly" (to "good").

- `seg-0030` · voice · low
  > with what we two have seen these last two nights.
  Padded, and it repeats "two". The source reads "What we two nights have seen."
  Fix: what we two nights have seen.

- `seg-0247` · voice · low
  > as thought, or the imaginings of love,
  An unneeded rewrite; "meditation or the thoughts of love" is clear.
  Fix: as meditation or the thoughts of love,

- `seg-0205` · voice · low
  > revisit the moonlight like this,
  "The glimpses of the moon" is flattened. The phrase is clear in context.
  Fix: revisit thus the glimpses of the moon,

- whole act · form · low
  Every verse line after the first in a speech now starts lower-case, where the source capitalizes each line. This is a choice, but it makes the page look like prose broken at odd points. Decide it once for the whole work and record it in the edition's notes.
  Also mixed: "Oh, all you host of heaven! O earth!" (`seg-0252`). Pick one rule: "O" for a vocative, "Oh" for an exclamation.

## Best lines
- `seg-0151`: "keep it locked still in your silence;" clears "tenable" and keeps the rhythm.
- `seg-0161`: "the fragrance and diversion of a minute;" is a light and accurate fix of "perfume and suppliance".
