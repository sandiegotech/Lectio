# The Epic of Gilgamesh · Tablet XI · The Flood, the Plant, and the Walls of Uruk
Grade: B
The flood narrative, the loaves and the snake are superb and should not be touched. Fix the boat's measurements, a contradiction about where Uta-napishti lives, stray quotation marks, and an editor's moral added after the closing lines.

No comparison edition is bundled. Fidelity points below are checked from knowledge of the Standard Babylonian text and are unverified against a bundled source.

## Issues
- `seg-0019` · fidelity · high
  > This is the wall he came home to, the one thing that would stand. He had gone to the ends of the earth and come back with empty hands
  The epic ends on Gilgamesh's own words about the wall, which repeat the prologue: the measures of Uruk. Everything after them is the editor's interpretation ("the only kind a man is given to keep", "And so the story ends where it began") written in as the last lines of the epic. The source leaves the reader to make the connection; the edition makes it for them.
  Fix: end the epic at "...three square miles and a half, and all of it is Uruk.”" and cut the rest of the segment.
- `seg-0004` · fidelity · medium
  > its sides rose ten times the height of a man; the top of each side ran a hundred and twenty cubits across, and it was a square.
  In the SB the walls are ten rods (120 cubits) high and each side of the deck is ten rods: the boat is a cube. "Ten times the height of a man" is about half that, so the shape is lost. Unverified against a bundled source.
  Fix: "Its walls rose a hundred and twenty cubits high, and each side of its deck ran a hundred and twenty cubits: as high as it was long and wide."
- `seg-0013` · fidelity · medium
  > Uta-napishti shall live far off, at the source of the rivers.' And they took me, and they settled me far away, at the mouth of the rivers, where the waters begin.
  The same place is called both "source" and "mouth", and "where the waters begin" adds to the confusion. The SB has "at the mouth of the rivers" in both lines. Unverified against a bundled source.
  Fix: "...shall live far off, at the mouth of the rivers.' And they took me and settled me far away, at the mouth of the rivers."
- `seg-0015` · form · medium
  > "So she baked the loaves and set them by his head
  The segment is narration, but it opens with a double quotation mark as if Uta-napishti were still speaking; the dialogue inside then switches to single quotes. Uta-napishti's story ends in seg-0014. Tablets X and XI also use straight quotes (") while Tablets I–IX use curly quotes (“ ”).
  Fix: "So she baked the loaves..." with no opening mark; use “ ” for Gilgamesh's and Uta-napishti's speech here; normalize Tablets X–XI to curly quotes.
- `seg-0002` · fidelity · medium
  > And Ea, the clever one, had sworn the oath with them, so he could not warn me himself. But he said the words to the reed wall of my house. He spoke to the fence of reeds, so that he had not broken his oath to a man
  The source says only that Ea was under oath with them and repeated their words to the reed fence. The edition explains the trick twice ("so he could not warn me himself", "so that he had not broken his oath to a man"). Let the reader see it in the speech that follows.
  Fix: "Ea, the clever one, had sworn the oath with them, but he repeated their words to the reed wall of my house."
- `seg-0003` · fidelity · low
  > And Ea taught me what to tell them.
  The SB gives Ea's cover story in full: say Enlil hates you, so you are going down to live with Ea in the deep, and Enlil will rain down plenty on the city, bread at dawn and a shower of wheat at dusk. That double-edged promise (the plenty is the flood) is dropped and reduced to a summary. Unverified against a bundled source.
  Fix: "Ea said to me: 'Tell them this: Enlil hates me, so I cannot live in your city or set foot on Enlil's ground. I will go down to the deep and live with Ea my lord. And he will rain down plenty on you: at dawn a shower of bread, and at dusk a rain of wheat.'"
- `seg-0007` · fidelity · low
  > And Ishtar cried out like a woman in childbirth, the sweet-voiced Lady of the gods wailed aloud
  The source has two names in parallel: Ishtar, and sweet-voiced Belet-ili. The second name is replaced with a title, though Belet-ili is the one who later lifts the lapis flies (seg-0011).
  Fix: "...and sweet-voiced Belet-ili, the Lady of the gods, wailed aloud"
- `seg-0008` · read-aloud · low
  > It was still as still.
  Reads as a slip, not an idiom.
  Fix: "Everything was still."
