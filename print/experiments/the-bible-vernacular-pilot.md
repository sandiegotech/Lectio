# The Bible — A Vernacular Edition
### Pilot: Genesis 1–3 · Psalm 23 · Mark 1

*An SDIT Vernacular experiment for Lectio. Not part of the published corpus; not reviewed.
See Method & Rights at the end of this document.*

---

## Translator's Preface

The Bible has always moved by translation. Jesus taught in Aramaic, the everyday speech of
Galilee; the Gospels that carry his words were written in Koine — "common" — Greek, the
dialect of markets and letters, not of literature. Jerome's Latin was called the *Vulgata*,
the common version. Tyndale gave the text plain English and was executed for it; the King
James Version, which later generations mistook for the voice of heaven itself, was a
committee of scholars doing exactly what this edition does — rendering an ancient text in
the strongest living language they had. The archaism came later, by aging. This edition
continues the pattern, not against the tradition but inside it: the same book, in the
language the reader actually thinks in.

Two commitments govern the rendering. First, this is a synthesis of the whole tradition —
worked from the Hebrew and Greek alongside the public-domain English line (Tyndale to the
American Standard Version), with the modern scholarly tradition consulted at every disputed
point — not a paraphrase of any one Bible. Second, the weight stays. The Hebrew of Genesis
is spare, repetitive, and strange; Mark's Greek is rough, breathless, and fast; Psalm 23 is
six verses of perfect economy. Where older English Bibles smoothed these textures into one
continuous churchly voice, this edition keeps each register: the refrains of Genesis stay
refrains, Mark keeps its "and… and… right away" urgency, and nothing anywhere is padded,
preached, or explained. Where the Hebrew or Greek is genuinely disputed, the reading
follows the argument, not the habit — and the Translation Notes at the end say why.

## Genesis 1–2:3 — The Seven Days

1 When God began to create the heavens and the earth — 2 the earth was a shapeless waste,
darkness lay over the face of the deep, and the breath of God hovered over the waters —
3 God said, "Let there be light." And there was light. 4 God saw that the light was good,
and God separated the light from the darkness. 5 God called the light Day, and the darkness
he called Night. And there was evening, and there was morning: the first day.

6 God said, "Let there be a vault in the middle of the waters, to separate water from
water." 7 So God made the vault, and it separated the water beneath it from the water above
it. And so it was. 8 God called the vault Sky. And there was evening, and there was
morning: the second day.

9 God said, "Let the waters under the sky be gathered into one place, so that dry land can
appear." And so it was. 10 God called the dry land Earth, and the gathered waters he called
Seas. And God saw that it was good. 11 God said, "Let the earth put out growth: plants that
seed themselves, and every kind of fruit tree bearing fruit with its seed inside it." And
so it was. 12 The earth put out growth — every kind of seed-bearing plant, every kind of
tree bearing fruit with its seed inside it. And God saw that it was good. 13 And there was
evening, and there was morning: the third day.

14 God said, "Let there be lights in the vault of the sky to separate day from night. Let
them mark the seasons, the days, and the years, 15 and let them shine in the vault of the
sky to light the earth." And so it was. 16 God made the two great lights — the greater
light to govern the day, the lesser light to govern the night — and the stars. 17 God set
them in the vault of the sky to light the earth, 18 to govern day and night, and to
separate light from darkness. And God saw that it was good. 19 And there was evening, and
there was morning: the fourth day.

20 God said, "Let the waters swarm with living creatures, and let birds fly over the earth,
across the vault of the sky." 21 So God created the great sea creatures, and every kind of
living thing that moves and swarms in the water, and every kind of winged bird. And God saw
that it was good. 22 God blessed them: "Be fruitful and multiply. Fill the waters of the
seas — and let the birds multiply on the earth." 23 And there was evening, and there was
morning: the fifth day.

24 God said, "Let the earth bring forth every kind of living creature: livestock, crawling
things, and every kind of wild animal." And so it was. 25 God made every kind of wild
animal, every kind of livestock, and everything that crawls on the ground. And God saw that
it was good.

26 Then God said, "Let us make humanity in our image, after our likeness, and let them hold
sway over the fish of the sea, the birds of the sky, the livestock, the whole earth, and
everything that crawls on it." 27 So God created humanity in his own image — in the image
of God he created it; male and female he created them. 28 God blessed them, and God said to
them, "Be fruitful and multiply. Fill the earth and master it. Hold sway over the fish of
the sea, the birds of the sky, and every living thing that moves on the earth." 29 And God
said, "Look — I have given you every seed-bearing plant on the face of the earth, and every
tree with seed in its fruit. They are yours to eat. 30 And to every wild animal, every bird
of the sky, and everything that crawls on the earth with the breath of life in it, I give
every green plant for food." And so it was. 31 And God saw all that he had made — and look,
it was very good. And there was evening, and there was morning: the sixth day.

**2** 1 So the heavens and the earth were finished, and everything in them. 2 On the seventh
day God finished the work he had done, and on the seventh day he rested from all his work.
3 God blessed the seventh day and made it holy, because on it he rested from all the work
of creating he had done.

## Genesis 2:4–3:24 — The Garden

4 This is the story of the heavens and the earth when they were created.

On the day the LORD God made earth and heavens, 5 no shrub of the field was yet on the
earth and no plant of the field had sprouted — for the LORD God had not sent rain on the
earth, and there was no human to work the soil — 6 but a mist would rise from the earth
and water the whole face of the ground. 7 Then the LORD God formed the man from the dust
of the ground, and breathed the breath of life into his nostrils, and the man became a
living being.

*[Trans. note: In Hebrew the wordplay is total — God forms the "human" (adam) from the
"humus" (adamah). The man is named for the dirt he comes from, and the whole story of 3:19
— "to dust you will return" — is folded into his name.]*

8 The LORD God planted a garden in Eden, in the east, and there he placed the man he had
formed. 9 And out of the ground the LORD God grew every tree that is pleasing to look at
and good to eat from — with the tree of life in the middle of the garden, and the tree of
the knowledge of good and evil.

10 A river flows out of Eden to water the garden, and from there it divides into four
branches. 11 The first is named Pishon; it winds through the whole land of Havilah, where
there is gold — 12 and the gold of that land is good; bdellium is there, and lapis lazuli.
13 The second river is named Gihon; it winds through the whole land of Cush. 14 The third
river is named Tigris; it runs east of Assyria. And the fourth river is the Euphrates.

15 The LORD God took the man and set him in the garden of Eden to work it and to watch
over it. 16 And the LORD God commanded the man: "You may eat freely from every tree in the
garden — 17 but from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil you may not eat. The day
you eat from it, you die."

18 Then the LORD God said, "It is not good for the man to be alone. I will make him a
partner who matches him." 19 So the LORD God formed from the ground every wild animal and
every bird of the sky, and brought them to the man to see what he would call them; and
whatever the man called each living creature, that was its name. 20 The man gave names to
all the livestock, to the birds of the sky, and to every wild animal. But for the man
himself, no matching partner was found. 21 So the LORD God put the man into a deep sleep,
and while he slept, took one of his sides and closed up the flesh in its place. 22 And the
LORD God built the side he had taken from the man into a woman, and brought her to the
man. 23 And the man said:

> "This one, at last — bone of my bones, flesh of my flesh.
> This one will be called Woman, for from Man she was taken."

24 That is why a man leaves his father and mother and holds fast to his wife, and they
become one flesh. 25 And the two of them were naked, the man and his wife, and they felt
no shame.

*[Trans. note: "Side," not the traditional "rib" — the Hebrew word (tsela) means the side
of a thing everywhere else it appears: the side of the ark, of the altar, of a building.
The woman is built from the man's side, not from a spare part. The older reading entered
through the Greek and stuck.]*

**3** 1 Now the serpent was the most cunning of all the wild animals the LORD God had made.
He said to the woman, "Did God really say you can't eat from any tree in the garden?"
2 The woman said to the serpent, "We may eat the fruit of the trees in the garden. 3 It's
only the fruit of the tree in the middle of the garden that God said, 'Don't eat it —
don't even touch it — or you will die.'" 4 The serpent said to the woman, "You will not
die. 5 God knows that the day you eat from it your eyes will be opened, and you will be
like gods, knowing good and evil." 6 And the woman saw that the tree was good to eat from,
and a delight to the eyes, and desirable for the wisdom it would give. She took its fruit
and ate; and she gave some to her husband beside her, and he ate. 7 Then the eyes of the
two of them were opened, and they knew they were naked; and they sewed fig leaves together
and made themselves loincloths.

*[Trans. note: The Hebrew rhymes "naked" (arummim, 2:25) against "cunning" (arum, 3:1) —
the last word before the serpent enters and the first word about him. No English pair
carries it; hear it as the story's hinge.]*

8 They heard the sound of the LORD God walking in the garden in the evening breeze, and
the man and his wife hid from the LORD God among the trees of the garden. 9 The LORD God
called to the man: "Where are you?" 10 He said, "I heard the sound of you in the garden,
and I was afraid because I was naked, so I hid." 11 And he said, "Who told you that you
were naked? Have you eaten from the tree I commanded you not to eat from?" 12 The man
said, "The woman you put beside me — she gave me fruit from the tree, and I ate." 13 The
LORD God said to the woman, "What have you done?" And the woman said, "The serpent tricked
me, and I ate."

14 So the LORD God said to the serpent:

> "Because you did this, you are cursed beyond all livestock and all wild animals.
> On your belly you will go, and dust you will eat, all the days of your life.
> 15 I will set hatred between you and the woman, between your offspring and hers.
> He will strike your head, and you will strike his heel."

16 To the woman he said:

> "I will make your pains in childbirth great; in pain you will bear children.
> Your desire will be for your husband, and he will rule over you."

17 And to the man he said:

> "Because you listened to your wife and ate from the tree I commanded you not to eat
> from, the ground is cursed because of you. In hard labor you will eat from it all the
> days of your life. 18 Thorns and thistles it will grow for you, and you will eat the
> plants of the field. 19 By the sweat of your face you will eat bread, until you return
> to the ground — for from it you were taken. You are dust, and to dust you will return."

20 The man named his wife Eve, because she became the mother of all the living. 21 And the
LORD God made garments of skin for the man and his wife, and clothed them.

22 Then the LORD God said, "Look — the man has become like one of us, knowing good and
evil. Now he must not be allowed to reach out his hand and take from the tree of life too,
and eat, and live forever." 23 So the LORD God sent him out of the garden of Eden, to work
the ground he had been taken from. 24 He drove the man out; and east of the garden of Eden
he stationed the cherubim, and a sword of flame turning every way, to guard the road to
the tree of life.

## Mark 1 — The Beginning of the Good News

1 The beginning of the good news of Jesus Christ, the Son of God.

2 As it stands written in the prophet Isaiah —

> "Look, I am sending my messenger ahead of you; he will prepare your road.
> 3 A voice shouts in the wilderness: Get the road ready for the Lord. Make his paths
> straight."

4 — John appeared, baptizing in the wilderness and announcing a baptism of turning back,
for the forgiveness of sins. 5 The whole Judean countryside went out to him, and all the
people of Jerusalem; they were baptized by him in the Jordan River, confessing their sins.
6 John wore camel's hair with a leather belt at his waist, and he ate locusts and wild
honey. 7 And this is what he announced: "After me comes one stronger than I am. I'm not
fit to stoop down and untie the strap of his sandals. 8 I baptized you with water. He will
baptize you with the Holy Spirit."

9 In those days Jesus came from Nazareth in Galilee and was baptized by John in the
Jordan. 10 And right away, as he came up out of the water, he saw the sky torn open and
the Spirit coming down on him like a dove. 11 And a voice came from the sky: "You are my
Son, the one I love. In you I take delight."

12 And right away the Spirit drove him out into the wilderness. 13 He was in the
wilderness forty days, tested by Satan. He was with the wild animals, and angels looked
after him.

14 After John was arrested, Jesus came into Galilee announcing the good news of God:
15 "The time is up. The kingdom of God has come near. Turn your lives around, and trust
the good news."

16 Passing along the Sea of Galilee, he saw Simon, and Simon's brother Andrew, casting a
net into the sea — they were fishermen. 17 Jesus said to them, "Follow me, and I will make
you fishers of people." 18 And right away they left their nets and followed him. 19 Going
on a little farther, he saw James the son of Zebedee and his brother John in their boat,
mending the nets. 20 Right away he called them; and they left their father Zebedee in the
boat with the hired men, and went after him.

21 They went into Capernaum, and as soon as the Sabbath came he went into the synagogue
and taught. 22 And the people were astonished at his teaching, because he taught them like
someone with authority — not like the scribes. 23 Just then a man with an unclean spirit
was there in their synagogue, and he screamed: 24 "What do you want with us, Jesus of
Nazareth? Have you come to destroy us? I know who you are — the Holy One of God." 25 Jesus
cut him off: "Quiet. Come out of him." 26 The unclean spirit convulsed the man, shrieked,
and came out of him. 27 Everyone was stunned, and they argued about it: "What is this? A
new teaching — with authority. He gives orders even to the unclean spirits, and they obey
him." 28 And right away the report of him went out everywhere, through the whole
surrounding country of Galilee.

29 As soon as they left the synagogue, they went to the house of Simon and Andrew, with
James and John. 30 Simon's mother-in-law was lying down with a fever, and right away they
told him about her. 31 He went to her, took her hand, and lifted her up. The fever left
her, and she waited on them.

32 That evening, after sunset, they brought him everyone who was sick or held by demons —
33 the whole town was gathered at the door. 34 He healed many who were sick with every
kind of disease, and drove out many demons. And he would not let the demons speak, because
they knew him.

35 Very early, while it was still dark, he got up, went out to a deserted place, and
prayed there. 36 Simon and his companions hunted him down, 37 and when they found him they
said, "Everyone is looking for you." 38 He said to them, "Let's go somewhere else — to the
neighboring towns — so I can preach there too. That's what I came out to do." 39 And he
went through all of Galilee, preaching in their synagogues and driving out demons.

40 A leper came to him, begging him on his knees: "If you want to, you can make me clean."
41 Moved to the depths, Jesus stretched out his hand, touched him, and said, "I want to.
Be clean." 42 Right away the leprosy left him, and he was clean. 43 Jesus sent him off at
once with a stern warning: 44 "See that you say nothing to anyone. Just go show yourself
to the priest, and offer for your cleansing what Moses commanded — as proof for them."
45 But the man went out and started talking freely, spreading the story — so that Jesus
could no longer enter a town openly, but stayed out in deserted places. And people kept
coming to him from everywhere.

## Translation Notes

### The tradition consulted

The English Bible is the deepest translation tradition in the language. The line runs:
Wycliffe (1382, from the Latin) → **Tyndale** (1526–30, the fountainhead — most of the
King James New Testament is Tyndale's) → Geneva (1560, Shakespeare's Bible) → **KJV**
(1611) → Revised/American Standard (1885/1901) → RSV (1952) → Jerusalem (1966) → NEB/REB
(1970/89) → NIV (1978) → **NRSV** (1989/2021, the scholarly standard) → ESV (2001). Beside
the committee line stand the modern single-author renderings that matter most to this
edition's method: **Robert Alter** (Hebrew Bible, 2018 — the literary landmark; rhythm and
concreteness; the refusal to explain), **Everett Fox** (Torah, 1995 — Hebrew orality and
wordplay), **David Bentley Hart** (New Testament, 2017 — deliberately rough where the
Greek is rough), and **Sarah Ruden** (Gospels, 2021 — a classicist's fresh ear). Eugene
Peterson's *The Message* marks the boundary this edition must not cross: when the
rendering starts sounding like Peterson, it has slid from translation into paraphrase.

### Key decisions

**"When God began to create" (Gen 1:1).** The traditional "In the beginning God created
the heavens and the earth" reads verse 1 as a complete sentence. The Hebrew syntax more
likely opens a dependent clause resolved in verse 3 — creation begins not with a
pronouncement but with a scene already dark and wet, into which God speaks. Rashi read it
this way in the eleventh century; most modern scholarship agrees. This edition follows the
grammar. ("Let there be light," by contrast, is untouchable — not out of reverence for the
KJV, but because it is the exact, plain rendering of the three Hebrew words.)

**"The LORD" (small capitals).** Where the Hebrew writes the four-letter divine name YHWH
— a name, not a title, whose pronunciation was lost on purpose — English Bibles have
substituted "the LORD" in capitals since Tyndale. This edition keeps the convention: it is
honest (the capitals mark a substitution) without pretending to a pronunciation the
tradition deliberately gave up.

**"A shapeless waste" (Gen 1:2).** The Hebrew *tohu wabohu* is a rhyming pair meaning
something like unformed-and-empty; the KJV's "without form, and void" is accurate but
bloodless. This edition renders the sense and lets "the deep" (*tehom*) carry the dread.
The "breath of God" hovering: the word (*ruach*) means breath, wind, and spirit at once,
and the verb is used elsewhere of an eagle fluttering over its young — a brooding bird,
not a ghost.

**"Partner who matches him" / "side" (Gen 2).** The traditional "help meet"/"helper"
undersells the Hebrew *ezer kenegdo* — *ezer* is used most often of God himself, and
carries no subordination; *kenegdo* means corresponding-to-him, his counterpart. And the
woman is built from the man's *side* (*tsela*), the word used for the side of the ark and
the altar — "rib" entered through the Greek translation and stuck for two thousand years.

**Psalm 23.** "I shall not want" now misleads — *want* meant *lack* in 1611. "The valley
of the shadow of death" (*tsalmavet*) is probably "deep darkness," but death is inside the
Hebrew word, so this edition keeps it in the modern grammar: "a valley dark as death."
"Goodness and kindness" render *hesed* — the Bible's great untranslatable, the loyal love
that keeps covenant — and they *chase* the psalmist (*radaph*, the verb used for pursuing
enemies): grace here is not following you; it is hunting you. And the final "forever" is
not in the Hebrew, which says "for length of days" — "my whole life long."

**Mark's voice.** Mark is the rawest Greek in the New Testament: paratactic (*and… and…
and*), breathless, with "immediately" (*euthys*) more than forty times in sixteen chapters.
Every polished English Bible has smoothed this; this edition keeps it, because the
roughness is the urgency, and the American plain style — short declaratives, sentences
starting with *And* — happens to fit Mark better than it fits any other ancient text. So:
the sky is "torn open" at the baptism (Mark's violent word, which Matthew and Luke both
soften to "opened"); the Spirit "drove him out" into the wilderness (the same verb used
for driving out demons); and "right away" keeps drumming, as structural as Homer's
epithets.

**"Good news," "turn your lives around."** *Euangelion* meant a herald's announcement — a
victory, an accession — before it hardened into the church word "gospel"; this edition
uses "good news" because that is what a first hearer heard. *Metanoia*, traditionally
"repent," means a turning of the mind and life — "turn back," "turn your lives around" —
without the sawdust-trail baggage. "Son of Man" stays "Son of Man": it is a title, and
titles, like proper names, are load-bearing. "Fishers of people" keeps Tyndale's play
without the 1611 gender default.

**One textual honesty note.** "The Son of God" in Mark 1:1 is missing from some of the
earliest manuscripts; the leper story in 1:41 reads "moved with compassion" in most
manuscripts but "moved with anger" in a few important ones. This edition prints the
majority readings; a full edition would carry both facts in its apparatus, because
provenance is a Lectio principle and manuscripts are provenance.

## Method & Rights

Worked from the public-domain line — KJV (1611), American Standard Version (1901), Young's
Literal (1898), JPS (1917) — against the public-domain Hebrew (Leningrad Codex tradition)
and Greek (Westcott-Hort / SBLGNT lineage), with the modern copyrighted tradition (Alter,
Fox, Hart, Ruden, NRSV, REB) consulted as scholarship on disputed choices, never copied.
The rendering is Lectio's own. Like every Lectio edition: derived from public-domain
sources, provenance recorded, not a historical translation.

**Scale, honestly:** the full Protestant canon is ~780,000 words across 66 books — roughly
forty times this pilot. The vehicle for the full corpus is the overnight Vernacular loop
that produced the Republic, Analects, Confessions, and The Prince editions, working
book-by-book (Mark first, then Genesis, then Psalms) with this pilot as the register
standard. The collections already hold the shelves waiting for it: *Sacred Writings*, the
Tanakh, and the Bible shells in the Religious Texts group.
