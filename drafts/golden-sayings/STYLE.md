# The Golden Sayings of Epictetus · style sheet

Written before the first section. Every drafter and reviewer reads it. It agrees with the
Policies of the Lectio *Meditations* (`review/2026-11-17/meditations/rejected.md`) and with
the wording that edition already prints, because the two books stand side by side and Marcus
quotes this one.

## Register

Plain, current, never fashionable; readable from fifteen to ninety. Epictetus talks. He is
a teacher in a classroom, quick, blunt, funny, fond of the short question and the shorter
answer, and Arrian took him down as he spoke. The English is spoken English: short
sentences, "you", contractions avoided but nothing stiff. No thee and thou, no "hath",
"wouldst", "aught", "behold", "lo". No slang, nothing a reader of ninety would not say and
nothing a reader of fifteen would have to look up ("mindset", "triggered", "toxic", "own it"
are out; so are "perturbation", "equanimity", "vouchsafe", "husbandman").

- **No apparatus in a paragraph.** No brackets, footnotes, glosses, summaries, morals,
  ellipsis dots or italics. Crossley's notes, headings and bracketed Greek never enter.
- **No explanation written into the sentence.** A name stays bare (Rufus, Agrippinus,
  Lateranus). What a reader needs goes in `divergences` or the glossary.
- **Drafted from the Greek.** Crossley fixes which passage a saying is, where it begins and
  ends, and what he left out. The words come from Schenkl's text. Where Crossley softens,
  Christianizes ("the Godhead", "sin", "Thy Administration"), pads or turns a clause into a
  maxim, the Greek is followed.
- **Extent.** A saying begins and ends where Crossley's does. Where he cut a sentence in
  half, the Lectio gives the whole Greek sentence if the half cannot stand. Where he skipped,
  the Lectio skips, silently; the gap is in the concordance in SOURCES.md.
- **Hedges, questions and repetitions stay** as the Greek has them. Epictetus repeats a word
  three times on purpose; so does the English.

## Person and address

- A generic person is **"a man … he"**, plural **"people"** or "men" (Meditations policy 5).
  No singular "they". ἄνθρωπος as "human being" where the point is the species.
- Epictetus addressing his hearer: **"you"**. His vocatives are kept and always the same:
  ἄνθρωπε **"Man,"**; ἀνδράποδον **"slave"**; τάλας, ταλαίπωρε **"poor wretch"**; ὦ βέλτιστε
  "my good man"; ἄνδρες "gentlemen" only where he addresses the class, otherwise "men".
- Arrian's frame (ἔφη, φησί) is kept: "he said", "said Epictetus". φησί marking the imagined
  objector is "someone says" or "you say", or is carried by the quotation marks alone.
- God speaks and is spoken to as **"you"**, lower case; "he", "him", "his" lower case.

## The gods

- ὁ θεός **"God"**; οἱ θεοί **"the gods"**; ὁ Ζεύς, Ζεῦ **"Zeus"** (never Jupiter, never
  "God" for Zeus, never Zeus for θεός); τὸ θεῖον "the divine"; τὸ δαιμόνιον "the divine".
- **The daimon** (ὁ ἑκάστου δαίμων, I.14; saying XXXVII) is **"the divinity within"** (Meditations policy 3).
  Where a man blames his daimon for his lot (CXL) it is "his guardian divinity"; θεοὶ καὶ
  δαίμονες (CLXXXVIII) are "gods and divine powers". "Daemon", "demon", "genius" and
  "guardian spirit" are not used.
- πρόνοια **"providence"**, lower case. ἡ διοίκησις "the way the universe is governed", "his
  governance". ὁ κόσμος **"the universe"**; "world" only for the inhabited world.
- Oaths: νὴ Δία, νὴ τὸν Δία **"by Zeus"**; νὴ τοὺς θεούς **"by the gods"**; μὴ γένοιτο **"Far
  from it."** (not "God forbid", which the Greek does not say).

## Shared wording with the Meditations

- Fragment 26 (saying X) is printed exactly as Meditations IV.41 prints it: **"You are a
  little soul carrying around a corpse."**
- Where Epictetus and Marcus quote the same line (the *Crito* line on Anytus and Meletus,
  Cleanthes' "Lead me, Zeus"), the wording is fixed here and repeated wherever it recurs:
  **"Lead me, Zeus, and you, Destiny, to wherever you have long since assigned me."** /
  **"If this is what pleases the gods, so be it."** (Epictetus quotes it once with the
  singular, I.29.18, saying LII: "If this is what pleases God, so be it.") / **"Anytus and Meletus can kill me, but
  they cannot harm me."**
- Other repeated formulas, the same every time: τί οὖν; **"What then?"**; the door is open
  (ἤνοικται ἡ θύρα) **"the door is open"**; οὐκέτι παίξω **"I won't play any more"** (a child's
  words, and the one contraction in the book); κόσμιος **"a citizen of the universe"**.

## Names

Familiar Latinized forms, as in the Meditations: Socrates, Diogenes, Antisthenes, Zeno,
Cleanthes, Chrysippus, Epicurus, Epictetus, Heracles (the Greek form, with Zeus and
Asclepius), Odysseus, Achilles, Agamemnon. Romans in Latin: Rufus (Musonius Rufus is plain
"Rufus", as Epictetus calls him), Epaphroditus, Agrippinus, Helvidius Priscus, Vespasian,
Lateranus, Thrasea, Florus, Nero, Caesar. Places in their English forms: Rome, Athens,
Nicopolis, Olympia, Gyara.

## Things

Money and measures stay ancient and bare: obol, drachma, denarius. The games stay Greek
(Olympia, the pancration, the pentathlon); a "pancratiast" is not a boxer (Meditations,
rejected XII). Offices stay Roman: consul, tribune, praetor, senator, procurator. The purple
stripe on a senator's toga is "the purple stripe". A παιδαγωγός is "the slave who minds a
child". The Cynic is "the Cynic", capital C.

## Quotations, speakers, verse

- **Double quotation marks**, never single as primary (Meditations policies 8 and 9). The
  imagined objector's words are in double quotation marks; Epictetus's own answers are not.
  Where two named speakers trade lines (Socrates and a friend, Epictetus and the governor in
  Arrian's narration), both are quoted. A quick self-question that cannot be misread stays
  bare ("What then? Is that a small thing to you? Far from it.").
- A whole saying that is one speech of Epictetus is not wrapped in quotation marks.
- **Dialogue runs on inside the one paragraph.** No line breaks, no dash-per-speaker.
- After Arrian's "he said:" a speech of Epictetus is not quoted, and the other man's short
  replies inside it are (XXXIV, XXXVI, XXXIX). Where a whole saying is a quick exchange of
  one-line turns (LXXXI, CXVI) both sides are quoted.
- **Quoted verse runs on as prose** inside double quotation marks (Meditations policy 8); no
  slashes. The exception is section 10, the Hymn of Cleanthes, which is a poem: there each
  paragraph is one group of lines set as a single prose sentence or two, plain and unrhymed,
  without hexameter, without "Thou", and without Crossley's line numbers or his diction.
- Dashes are unspaced em dashes (Meditations policy 9), used sparingly.
- Numbers: the saying number opens the paragraph, roman, with a full stop ("XXV. ").

## Greek left in Greek

None, with one exception under Meditations policy 7 (a sentence that depends on a Greek
word): saying CLXXXIII, where Gellius quotes Epictetus's two words. They are named once,
transliterated without accents, **anechou** and **apechou**, and not glossed, because Gellius
does not gloss them: his own sentence has just explained them, and the English of that
sentence uses "bear" and "forbear".
