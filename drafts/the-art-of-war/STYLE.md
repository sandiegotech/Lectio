# The Art of War · style sheet

Written before the first section. Every drafter and reviewer reads it. It agrees with the
Policies of the Lectio *Tao Te Ching* and *Analects* (`review/2026-11-17/*/rejected.md`) and
with the wording those two editions already print.

## Register

Plain, current, never fashionable; readable from fifteen to ninety. Sun Tzu is terse,
parallel and dry, and the English is too: short sentences, the same shape repeated when the
Chinese repeats it, no ornament the Chinese does not have. No business or sports language
("leverage", "game plan", "win-win", "morale boost"), no modern military jargon ("logistics",
"intel", "tactical", "deploy", "operations", "flank" unless the Chinese says side). No
Edwardian either ("hearken", "ere", "thus do", "soldiery", "choleric").

- **No apparatus in a paragraph.** No brackets, footnotes, glosses, summaries or morals. Giles's
  commentary never enters. A name or a measure the reader may not know stays bare and is
  explained in `divergences` or left for the glossary.
- **No gloss inside the line** (TTC policy). 率然 is "the shuai-jan", and the next sentence, which
  is Sun Tzu's own, says what it is.
- **Hedges and rhetorical questions stay** as the Chinese has them.
- **Nothing added to make a saying complete.** Where the Chinese leaves the subject out, the
  English uses "you", an imperative, or "he" for the general. No invented "we must".

## Person

- 孫子曰 is **"Master Sun said:"** at the head of every chapter, as the Analects has "The Master
  said" and "Master Yu said". The author on the title page is **Sun Tzu**.
- The general is **"he"**. A general maxim may use **"you"**. Singular "they" is not used for
  the general or the enemy (TTC policy).
- 吾 / 我 are kept: **"I"** where Sun Tzu gives his own judgment (吾以此知勝負矣), **"we" / "our"**
  where 我 means our side against the enemy.
- 敵 is **"the enemy"**, singular, pronoun **"he"**. 人 as the other side is "the enemy" or
  "others"; 彼 … 己 is **"the other side … yourself"**.
- 故 is **"So"**, 是故 **"That is why"** or "For this reason". They are kept, since the chain of
  "so" is how the book argues; one may be dropped only where two stand in one sentence.

## What stays in the original language

- **li** (里), roman, no italics, no conversion: "a thousand li". The Analects prints "li" too.
- **shuai-jan**, Chinese names. Nothing else. No "yin and yang" (陰陽 in I is "dark and light",
  in IX "the sunny side … the shade"), no "ch'i", no "tao".
- Measures are put into plain English, never converted to modern units: 金 "pieces of gold";
  鍾 and 石 "measure" and "load"; 鎰 and 銖 "a pound" and "a grain"; 仞 "fathoms"; 駟 "four-horse
  chariots"; 乘 "chariots".

## Numbers, quotes, verse

- Numbers as words. The lists Sun Tzu counts ("first … second …") are run as sentences, not
  as numbered lists; no "(1)", no digits.
- Straight double quotes, single inside double (Analects policy). Quotation marks only where
  the Chinese quotes: the Book of Army Management (VII), the world's "Well done" (IV), the two "May I ask" exchanges (XI),
  and named terms (XII 費留, XIII 神紀).
- No verse. Rhymed or four-beat passages (VII "swift as the wind", V "whirling and tumbling")
  are prose with the parallel kept.
- The em dash, spaced, is allowed but rare. No semicolon chains longer than the Chinese's own.
- Chapter labels "Chapter I" … "Chapter XIII". Titles translate the Chinese heading, short.
